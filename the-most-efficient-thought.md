# The Most Efficient Thought

*Essay 4 in the Vedanta and Science series · Ritam Wellbeing Programme, Ramakrishna Vedanta Centre New Zealand*

**Companion deck:** [The Most Efficient Thought](https://vedantanz.github.io/Course-lessons/the-most-efficient-thought.html)
**Published essay:** vedanta.nz

---

Patañjali makes a claim in the third chapter of the Yoga Sūtras that is easy to read past. Saṁyama on an object, he says, yields knowledge of that object. Not calm. Not pleasant absorption. Knowledge.

That is a strong claim, and the usual gloss on it, that the mind becomes clear, does not really explain anything. Clear how? Clearer in what respect, and why should sustained attention on a thing produce knowledge of it rather than merely a vivid impression of it? This essay offers an answer with some structure in it: saṁyama is an optimisation procedure, and what it optimises is the most fundamental unit of the mind. The answer matters in two directions at once, for anyone sitting down to practise, and for anyone thinking about what it means to refine a representation in any system at all.

Before reading on, spend a few minutes with [the presentation](https://vedantanz.github.io/Course-lessons/the-most-efficient-thought.html). The slide called **The Narrowing** is the whole argument in a single gesture: drag the slider and watch two peaks move together until they become one. The essay then gives the mechanism, the history and the caveats that a deck cannot hold.

## Where the earlier essays left us

Three pieces are already in place, and this essay leans on all three. If you have read them, this is a reminder. If you have not, it is enough to go on with.

*The Whole Message* introduced Dennis Gabor and a law he proved in 1946. A signal can be described in two complete ways, as something unfolding in time and as a recipe of steady tones, and you can never have both descriptions perfectly sharp at once. Strike a piano key for a split second and you get a definite *when* but a thud of blurred pitch. Hold the key and the pitch becomes definite while the *when* smears out. There is a floor on how sharp both can be together, and Gabor found the one shape that sits exactly on that floor, wasting nothing: a pure tone inside a smooth bell-shaped envelope. He called it a **logon**, and it is the most economical packet a bounded signal can be.

*The Settling Mind* took that picture into the mind. Every moment of experience, on the model this course works with, arises from two waves meeting. There is the **object-wave**, which is what is actually present, and the **attention-wave**, which is the state you bring to it. The two add together, and their sum has a hidden anatomy: a fast **carrier** at the average of the two frequencies, which carries the content of the moment, and a slow **envelope** governed by the gap between them, which carries the felt quality of the engagement. That whole resultant is what the Yoga tradition calls a **vṛtti**, a wave or turning of the mind.

*The Light and the Field* asked how any of this comes to be kept, following the parallel between holographic storage, the contemplative account of how impressions are retained, and how modern systems store what they have learned.

So we have the anatomy of a moment and an account of how it is recorded. What we have not yet asked is what a technique actually *does* to that anatomy.

## Two objects that look alike

Two objects have been doing the work so far, and there is a confusion between them that this series carried for a while and is correcting here. The correction is what makes the rest of this essay possible.

The first object is Gabor's logon: a tone inside a bell. The second is the beat of two tones, two steady frequencies added together, which also produces a carrier inside an envelope. They have the same silhouette, and it is natural to treat them as the same thing.

They are not the same thing, and they differ on precisely the property Gabor's paper was about. The logon is **bounded**. It fades in, it fades out, it occupies a patch of time and a patch of frequency, and it is as compact as anything can be. The two-tone beat, taken strictly as written, is **unbounded**. It is a pattern that has always been sounding and always will, with no beginning and no end. Its spectrum is not a localised bump but two infinitely sharp lines, one at each of the two original frequencies.

Once they are separated, each turns out to have been doing a different job. The beat models the **relational** anatomy of a moment: that it has two poles, that there is a gap between them, and that the story of practice is the story of that gap closing. The logon models the **episodic** anatomy: that a moment is bounded, and pays a price for being bounded at all.

Hold on to the difference, because what follows is the story of how one becomes the other.

## What a real moment has to be

A vṛtti arises, lasts, and passes. That is one of the first things the Yoga tradition says about it. Vivekananda's image is of a lake whose surface carries waves, and a wave is only the water taking a shape for a while before the water settles again. Whatever else a moment of knowing is, it is an episode.

So a real vṛtti carries both anatomies at once. It has two poles, and it has edges in time. Take the beat and hold it inside a bounded window, where the window is the arising and passing of the episode itself:

> R(t) = w(t) · [ A cos(2πf<sub>s</sub> t) + A cos(2πf<sub>m</sub> t) ]

The bracket is the two waves meeting, exactly as in *The Settling Mind*. The term w(t) is the episode's own envelope, fading in and fading out.

Something quietly satisfying happens here. Because adding and then windowing is the same as windowing and then adding, this object is identically **a pair of logons sharing one window**. The beat and the logon turn out to be one object with two dials rather than two rival pictures. The beat is what you get if you let the window run forever. A single logon is what you get if you close the gap to nothing. Everything in between, which is to say every actual moment of experience, is the general case that has been sitting between them the whole time.

That second dial is the one this essay is about.

## What saṁyama does

The Yoga tradition does not present dhāraṇā, dhyāna and samādhi as three separate practices. Patañjali gives them a single name when they are taken together on one object: **saṁyama**.

Recall what a vṛtti is on this picture. Two waves meet, and their sum has a carrier at the average of the two frequencies and an envelope governed by the gap between them. The carrier is what the moment is about. The envelope is the felt texture of the engagement: the flutter, the roughness, the sense of working at something.

Now ask what that envelope costs.

It costs part of the moment. A vṛtti carrying two frequencies has to spend some of its finite capacity on the difference between them. The beat is not a bonus feature of the experience. It is an internal difference the moment must carry *in addition to* carrying its object. The wider the gap, the more of the moment goes into the flutter rather than into the thing attended to. This is the ordinary condition of a mind: attending to something, and simultaneously registering its own difference from what it attends to, and mistaking the second for part of the first.

Saṁyama is the narrowing of that gap.

**Dhāraṇā** binds attention to one object, where before it was spread across many. Patañjali's word is *deśa-bandha*, binding to a place. In the model, this is what first makes a single gap meaningful: there is now one object-wave to have a gap with, rather than a dozen.

I. K. Taimni, in his 1961 commentary, describes this stage entirely in terms of the *rate of interruption*. The work of dhāraṇā, he writes, is to reduce progressively the frequency of interruptions and eventually to eliminate them. That is a rate-based description of practice, offered without any signal-processing intent, and it is exactly the variable our model cares about.

**Dhyāna** sustains the hold unbroken, and this is where the narrowing actually happens. Taimni's criterion is that the practitioner can continue without interruption for as long as he decides to. The attention-wave is not fixed. Held steadily against the same object, it drifts toward it. The gap closes, not because you force it closed, but because sustained contact is the condition under which two rhythms come into step.

### Why contact alone is enough

That last sentence deserves more than an assertion, because there is a well-studied phenomenon behind it, and it is one you have almost certainly witnessed without giving it a name.

In 1665, the Dutch physicist Christiaan Huygens lay ill in bed with two pendulum clocks hanging on the wall of his room. He noticed something odd. Within about half an hour, however the two pendulums had been started, they came to swing in exact opposition to each other, and they returned to that state even after he disturbed them. The clocks were not connected in any obvious way. What connected them was the wooden beam they both hung from, carrying vibrations so slight he could not feel them. That tiny channel was enough. Huygens called it an odd kind of sympathy.

Physicists call this **entrainment**, and it turns up wherever two oscillators with nearby frequencies have even the weakest link between them. It has two properties that matter here. The first is that it requires no force. Neither clock pushed the other. They drifted into a shared rhythm because a small coupling and a long time were present together. The second is that it requires nearness. Two rhythms far apart will never capture each other, however long you wait. There is a range inside which the pull toward synchrony is automatic, and outside which nothing happens at all.

You have seen this. Two people walking side by side fall into step, usually without either of them deciding to, and usually without noticing until someone points it out. It takes a minute or two of walking together. It cannot be hurried, and if you try to force it by watching your companion's feet, you will only stumble.

You have lived it. Your body keeps its own daily rhythm, and left to itself that rhythm runs a little longer than twenty-four hours. What holds it to the day is light. Morning light nudges the internal clock earlier, evening light nudges it later, and the correction happens without any effort or attention from you. Fly across eight time zones and the same mechanism shows its limits. The clock does not jump. It shifts by roughly an hour a day, which is why jet lag takes the better part of a week to clear. You cannot decide to be on Tokyo time. You can only put yourself in the light and wait.

Something looser happens between people over years. It is often said that birds of a feather flock together, and that is true, but it describes people finding each other because they were already alike. The more interesting observation is the one that comes afterwards: people who spend long enough in each other's company become more alike. They pick up each other's turns of phrase, their humour, their rhythms of speech, even what they find worth noticing. Nobody sets out to do this. It is what sustained proximity does.

None of these examples proves that a vṛtti behaves this way. Entrainment between clocks or between walkers is a relation between two systems in the world, and what we are describing is a relation between object and attention inside a single mind. That bridge is one this course draws, not a finding physics hands us. But it is worth noticing how exactly the signature matches. A mechanism that needs no force, that needs nearness, and that above all needs time, is precisely what the Yoga tradition's instructions have always implied. Do not strain. Come close. Stay.

There is also a consequence worth drawing out. If capture requires the two rhythms to be near each other already, then dhāraṇā is not merely preliminary tidying before the real work. It is what brings the attention-wave inside the range where dhyāna can do anything at all. The preparation is not separable from the refinement. It is its precondition.

## The word the traditions chose

There is a detail in the vocabulary itself worth pausing on, because it suggests the practitioners who built these practices understood something about their own mechanism.

Sanskrit has a prefix, **upa-**, meaning near, towards, alongside. Notice how much of the language of practice is built on it.

**Upāsanā**, the ordinary word for worship and contemplative practice, is *upa* plus the root *ās*, to sit. It means sitting near. **Upaniṣad**, the name given to the texts themselves, is sitting down near, in the posture of a student beside a teacher. **Upavāsa** is the standard word for fasting, and its parts are *upa* plus *vas*, to dwell: dwelling near. Whatever the abstention was for, the word insists it was not the point. **Upadeśa** is instruction, pointing something out from close at hand. **Upāya**, central in the Trika tradition, is the means, literally that by which one goes near. **Upanayana** is the initiation, the leading of a student near.

Not one of these is a word about conquest, or achievement, or force. They are all words about proximity and staying.

Set that beside what the physics says. Two rhythms come into step when they are near enough and given long enough, and not otherwise. Outside the capture range, effort accomplishes nothing at all.

We should be careful about what this shows. It does not show that anyone in the Yoga or Vedanta traditions knew about coupled oscillators. It shows something smaller and, in its way, more interesting: that when practitioners who had spent centuries observing what actually works came to name their practices, they named them after nearness and after staying.

## What samādhi arrives at

**Samādhi** is what the narrowing arrives at. Patañjali's description is precise: the mind becomes as though empty of its own form, and the object alone shines. In the model, that is what a gap of zero looks like. The envelope goes flat. The flutter stops. The two frequencies are no longer two. What remains is a single steady wave, held inside the window of the episode.

And that single wave in its window is a logon. Its time-bandwidth product sits exactly on the floor Gabor identified. Not near it. On it.

So the result can be stated in one sentence. **Saṁyama takes a vṛtti carrying two frequencies and drives it toward the most efficient unit of information a bounded episode can carry.**

![The narrowing: three panels for dhāraṇā, dhyāna and samādhi. Above, the waveform's envelope flutter slows and then flattens. Below, the spectrum's two peaks move together and merge into a single peak.](the-narrowing.png)

Nothing in that final moment is spent on internal difference. There is no gap to maintain, no flutter to register, no residue of the mind's own form colouring the presentation. The entire capacity of the episode goes into carrying the object. That is what it means for the object alone to shine, and it is why Patañjali's claim about knowledge is stronger than it first appears. A moment perfectly economical in carrying its object is one in which the maximum available information about that object is present, and the minimum is spent on anything else.

Taimni, again without any such intention, draws a picture of this. He represents the three stages as a series of successive moments, showing what the mind holds at regular intervals. In dhāraṇā, the object appears interspersed with distractions. In dhyāna, the object repeats unbroken, but each instance is circled to mark the self-awareness still accompanying it. In samādhi, the object repeats unbroken and the circles are gone. It is, without meaning to be, a sampling diagram of exactly the process described here, with the thinning of the circles standing for the closing of the gap.

This also changes how the practice looks. On this reading, saṁyama is not subtraction. It is not the removal of thought, or the suppression of the mind, or the emptying out of content. The object is still there at the end, fully present, more fully present than before. What has gone is the waste. What the practice removes is the portion of the moment that was being spent on the difference between knower and known, and what it leaves is the whole moment carrying the thing itself.

The Yoga tradition has a word for the mind in this condition, and it is not a word about emptiness. **Ekāgra**, one-peaked. A single spectral peak, where before there were two.

## Where the model stops

That word marks a boundary, and it is worth being exact about it rather than letting the mathematics imply more than it can carry.

Patañjali describes five states of mind, and ekāgra, one-peaked, is the fourth. The fifth is **niruddha**, restrained, and the definition of yoga given in the second sūtra of the whole text is *citta-vṛtti-nirodhaḥ*, the restraint of the modifications of the mind.

Everything this essay has described is the perfecting of a vṛtti. A single pure tone in a window is still a wave. It is the most efficient possible modification of the mind, and it is not the absence of modification. In the Yoga tradition's own vocabulary, driving the gap to zero arrives at ekāgratā and at *samprajñāta* samādhi, the state in which an object is still present and shining. What lies past it, where even that one remaining wave subsides, is not a further value of the gap. It is outside what this equation can represent at all.

This is a limit of the model, not of the path. The wave picture describes the refining of the instrument with some precision. It has nothing to say about setting the instrument down. We think it is better to mark that clearly than to let an elegant piece of mathematics seem to reach further than it does.

## What this might offer elsewhere

This essay has been about a mind. But the thing described is not, in its structure, specific to minds.

What saṁyama does, stated without any contemplative vocabulary, is this. It takes a representation that carries redundant internal structure, and refines it toward a minimal form in which nothing is spent on anything but the content. The refinement has three features worth noticing.

It is achieved by sustained contact rather than by force. The narrowing is not driven; it is what happens when two nearby rhythms are held in contact over time. Strain is not among the operative variables, and is in fact counterproductive, since strain is itself a fluctuation that widens the gap.

It requires prior nearness. Nothing happens at all outside the capture range, which means the preparatory work of getting the two into proximity is not separable from the refinement.

And its endpoint is not the removal of content. At the end the object is more fully present, not less. What has been removed is the overhead: the portion of the moment that was going into maintaining an internal difference rather than into carrying the thing itself.

We offer this as a description, not as a correspondence. We are not claiming that saṁyama matches any particular procedure by which representations in artificial systems are trained or compressed. What we are saying is narrower. Here is a well-developed account of what it means to optimise a unit of representation, arrived at from an entirely different direction over a very long period of time, by people whose method was to watch the process from inside rather than to build instruments to observe it from outside. It has a specific and somewhat unusual structure. Anyone thinking carefully about what it means for a representation to become efficient may find it worth sitting beside the accounts currently in use.

That is a structural bridge, in the terms this series uses. It is not a finding, and it is not testimony. It is an offer.

## Closing

In 1946, working on the problem of how much information a telephone line could carry, Dennis Gabor proved that there is a most efficient possible unit of a signal, and worked out its exact shape. He was not thinking about meditation. He was thinking about bandwidth.

Some two thousand years earlier, a tradition that knew nothing of signals or spectra had built a technique for producing that object in a human mind, described its stages in careful detail, and named it after the act of coming near and staying.

Neither knew about the other. That they arrive at the same shape does not prove anything about either one. But it is worth a moment's attention, and it suggests that when we ask what the mind is and how it may be refined, we are not asking an idle or a merely private question.

## A note on method

> This essay presents the mathematics and the history faithfully. Gabor's 1946 limit, the behaviour of weakly coupled oscillators, and Huygens' observation of his clocks are established results, and the account of that observation has been checked against the scholarly literature. The connections drawn here to contemplative practice, including the reading of the two waves as object and attention and of saṁyama as an optimisation of a vṛtti, are offered as structural bridges for insight, not as claims that the mathematics proves any metaphysics, nor that the Yoga tradition anticipated Gabor. What the Yoga tradition reports from the inside about the closing of the gap is first-person testimony, offered with respect, and it is not a third-person measurement.
