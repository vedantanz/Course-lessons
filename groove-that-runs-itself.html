The Ritam Wellbeing Programme · Essay five of the signal series

The Groove That Runs Itself

Four essays asked what a moment of experience is made of. This one asks a different question: what does everything that already happened to you do to the moment that is arriving now?

Written for anyone who wants to understand their own reactions, and also for anyone working on interpretability, character formation and alignment in artificial systems. The mechanism turns out to be the same one.

Move through with the arrows, the buttons below, or by swiping. The centre of this deck is a working model you can change with your own hands.

Something we read past

The bracket nobody opened

Here is the equation from the earlier essays. It says that a moment of experience is what the world sends, meeting the attention you bring, with some background noise added.

[ ψx ∗ h(t) ] + G(t)·ψm(t) + n(t)

We spent three essays on the meeting. We never opened the gold bracket.

The signal from the world does not arrive raw. Before it reaches the meeting at all, it has already passed through h(t). That single letter is the subject of this essay, and it turns out to be the most consequential term in the whole equation for how your day actually feels.

Tuesday afternoon

A remark about a roster

A nurse we will call Meera is three hours into a shift. A colleague says, without any edge in it, that the roster has been changed again and she is on Saturday.

Meera hears herself answer far more sharply than the moment deserved. The room goes slightly quiet. She spends the next forty minutes turning it over, composing apologies she does not send, and feeling the whole thing sit in her chest long after the conversation has moved on.

Nothing in that sentence about the roster warranted any of it.

The usual explanation is that Meera is stressed, or tired, or not coping. None of those is wrong. None of them explains the shape of what happened: a small input, a large output, and a long tail.

How engineers ask the question

Strike it once and listen

If you want to know what a system will do to anything you send through it, you do not send it everything. You send it one sharp tap and you watch what comes back.

Tap a tuning fork and it rings at its own pitch, for its own length of time, and then stops. Tap a cracked bell and you get something else. Tap a pillow and you get almost nothing. The tap is identical every time. What differs is the thing you tapped.

That response to a single tap is called the impulse response, and it is written h(t). It is a complete fingerprint. Once you know how a system answers one tap, you know how it will answer anything at all.

In plain words

The impulse response is not a record of what happened to the system. It is a description of how the system now behaves.

Which is also why understanding any trained system from the inside is hard. If the past is present only as a change in how something responds, you cannot open it up and read what happened. You can only tap it and watch.

What the science shows

The body does keep the tap

An experience does leave a physical trace. Learning changes the strength of connections between neurons, and the pattern of those changes is what memory researchers call an engram. The trace is largely inert until something cues it, and when it fires it reconstructs an experience rather than replaying a recording.

Repeated actions do something related but distinct. Circuits running through the basal ganglia turn a deliberate sequence into an automatic one, triggered by a cue rather than by a decision. The more it runs, the less it needs you.

And perception is not neutral before you get to it. Prior emotional associations measurably change what you notice and how you read it, before any conscious appraisal has happened.

Honesty line

Those three findings are established science. What follows in this deck, reading them alongside a contemplative account of the mind, is a structural model this course draws. It is offered as a bridge for insight, not as proof that either side validates the other.

The operation at the centre

Every arriving signal, reshaped by every echo

The little asterisk in the equation is not multiplication. It is convolution, and it describes something very specific.

Think of the world sending you a series of taps. Each one enters a system that is still ringing from the taps before it. What you actually experience is not the taps. It is the sum of all their ringings, overlapping.

Convolution is the precise way of saying: every incoming signal is reshaped by every echo of every signal that came before it.

On the next slide you can do this yourself. Send the taps. Change what they are passing through. Watch what comes out the other side.

What you just did

Three numbers, not three diagnoses

The four settings have names that belong to very different conversations. Rumination, reactivity, a background hum of unease: in ordinary speech these sound like separate problems, or separate kinds of person.

In the model they are the same filter with three dials in different positions.

  • Overshoot is a response larger than the thing that caused it. Meera's answer about the roster.
  • Settling time is how long it keeps going after the event has ended. The forty minutes of unsent apologies.
  • Residual ripple is activation with no trigger present at all. Waking already braced, before anything has happened.

This is not a claim that distress reduces to three numbers. It is a claim that the disproportion has a shape, and the shape is describable, and describable things can be worked with.

Now the tradition speaks

The vibration dies out. What is left?

In 1896, long before anyone had imaged a synapse, Swami Vivekananda was asked why the mind needs practice at all. His answer describes exactly what you have been watching.

Because each action is like the pulsations quivering over the surface of the lake. The vibration dies out, and what is left? The Saṁskāras, the impressions. Swami Vivekananda, Raja Yoga, on Yoga Sūtra I.12

The wave passes. The trace remains. That is the impulse response, stated in the vocabulary of water rather than engineering, a full half century before anyone wrote h(t) on a blackboard.

The Sanskrit word is saṁskāra: an impression left behind by experience.

Yoga Sūtra I.11

An impression is not yet a thought

अनुभूतविषयासम्प्रमोषः स्मृतिः

anubhūta-viṣaya-asampramoṣaḥ smṛtiḥ

Memory is when the Vṛttis of perceived subjects do not slip away, and through impressions come back to consciousness. Vivekananda's rendering

Read the second half carefully. The impression is not the memory. The impression is what makes the memory possible. Between the original experience and its return, the trace sits dormant, doing nothing you could observe.

Taimni's commentary sharpens it: as long as impressions are present in their potential form, they cannot be considered a vṛtti, a movement of the mind. They are loaded, not firing.

In plain words

What the past leaves in you is not a stored picture. It is a change in how you will answer the next thing that happens.

Vivekananda on the brain

Each thought makes a street

He pushes the image further, and this time the vocabulary is startlingly physical.

Each thought that we have makes a street, as it were, in the brain … Human nature likes to run through the ruts that are already there, because it is easy. Swami Vivekananda, Raja Yoga

The groove is his word, not a modern import. And the reason he gives for why we keep using the old ones is not a moral failing. It is simply that they are easier. A path that already exists costs less to travel.

Which is exactly what your reactions feel like from the inside. Not chosen. Just the way the ground already runs.

Where suffering enters

Named in the sūtra itself

This is not a neutral piece of mental machinery. Patañjali lists the sources of duḥkha, the ache in things, and puts saṁskāra among them directly, alongside the pain of change and the anxiety of wanting.

pariṇāma-tāpa-saṁskāra-duḥkhaiḥ … (II.15)

The reason is visible in what you were just moving. A filter with long settling and high overshoot does not only distort one afternoon. It distorts every afternoon, because every incoming signal passes through it, and it is still ringing from the last one.

You are not reacting to today. You are reacting to today plus everything still echoing.

The turn

Some are painful and some are not

वृत्तयः पंचतय्यः क्लिष्टा अक्लिष्टाः

There are five classes of modifications, some painful and others not painful. Yoga Sūtra I.5, Vivekananda's rendering

That short line is doing more work than it appears to. It rules out, from the fifth sūtra of the text, the idea that conditioning as such is the enemy.

Kliṣṭa and akliṣṭa: afflicted and unafflicted. The tradition is not asking you to become a mind with no impressions in it. It is asking a much more practical question about which ones are running.

Grooves you would not give up

The musician's hands

A pianist does not decide where the fingers go. Twenty years of practice laid down a filter, and now the playing runs itself while attention goes somewhere higher.

A nurse recognises the patient who is deteriorating before the numbers say so. A parent wakes at a change in a child's breathing. Someone reliably kind is not calculating kindness each time; the response is simply the shape of their filter.

Every one of those is a groove, formed the same way, by repetition.

The question was never whether to have a filter. It is what is in the one you have.

What this is good for

Your reaction is not your verdict

Here is the part that matters on a hard afternoon.

When Meera snapped, the conclusion that arrived with it was about her: that she is someone who does this, that the sharpness revealed what she really thinks. That conclusion is the most painful part of the episode and it is the least reliable thing in it.

An overshooting response is a statement about the filter. It is not a statement about the worth of the person the filter belongs to, and it is not a considered judgment about a colleague.

This is not an excuse, and the tradition never offers it as one. It is something more useful. It moves the question from what kind of person am I to what is running, and can it be changed. Only the second of those has an answer you can act on.

The answer, stated plainly

Counter habits

The only remedy for bad habits is counter habits … Character is repeated habits, and repeated habits alone can reform character. Swami Vivekananda, Raja Yoga

Not suppression. Not removal. Replacement, by the same mechanism that laid the first groove down.

How that works in detail, the moment of stillness between an old impression subsiding and a new one arising, is the third essay in this short series. What Vivekananda will not let us leave without is the consequence:

Never say any man is hopeless, because he only represents a character, a bundle of habits, which can be checked by new and better ones. Swami Vivekananda, Raja Yoga

One thread to pick up later

Other things get measured this way too

When researchers want to understand what is going on inside an artificial neural network, one of the instruments they reach for is the Jacobian: the system's sensitivity, at a particular point, to a small tap. It is doing the same job h(t) does here, and it can be read for how much a given direction gets amplified, which is the quantity we have been calling overshoot.

Three problems in that field turn on exactly this material. Interpretability is the difficulty of reading a disposition that exists only as behaviour. Character formation is how a consistent way of responding gets laid down, and by what. Alignment includes whether an unwanted disposition can be removed without damaging the system that has it.

That last one is the question Vivekananda answers with counter habits, and which Patañjali treats in his third chapter as the replacement of one impression by another.

None of this claims that such a system has impressions, or experience, or anything like an inner life. It is the observation that a mechanism first described in the Yoga Sūtras turns up, in recognisable form, in something built for entirely unrelated reasons.

Where the correspondence holds, and exactly where it stops, is the seventh essay.

What to carry out of this

Your reaction arrived before you did. It came through a filter that the past built, and it says more about the filter than about you.

  • An experience leaves an impression, not a recording. The impression is dormant until something rouses it.
  • What it changes is your response, and the change shows up as gain, as duration, and as background activation.
  • Disproportion is mechanical before it is moral.
  • The filter is also where skill, warmth and steadiness live. It is not to be dismantled.
  • It is changed by repetition, which is the way it was made.
